The River

Natural history, geology, wildlife, and the legends of Cataract Canyon

Geology: Two Billion Years in the Walls

Cataract Canyon cuts through the Colorado Plateau, exposing rock layers that span nearly two billion years of Earth history. The canyon walls rise as high as 1,400 feet, revealing red Wingate sandstone, buff-colored Navajo sandstone, and the dark, metamorphic Vishnu basement rocks far below the waterline. The Colorado and Green rivers carved this gorge over the past five to six million years as the plateau slowly uplifted — a geologic race between erosion and uplift that the river has, so far, won.

The canyon sits at the confluence of the Colorado and Green rivers, just 14 miles below the point where those two great western rivers merge. From that confluence downstream, the river drops more steeply, generating the rapids that give Cataract Canyon its reputation.

The Rapids

Cataract Canyon holds 28 named rapids in roughly 14 miles — one of the highest concentrations of significant whitewater in North America. At high water in late spring, several of the lower rapids merge into a continuous roar known simply as "the Big Drops." Rapids 21, 22, and 23 — Big Drop 1, 2, and 3 — are among the most powerful commercially runnable rapids on the continent.

Water levels in May can vary dramatically depending on snowpack in the Rockies. At high flows (above 30,000 cfs), the Big Drops form massive hydraulics and standing waves exceeding 20 feet. At moderate flows, the technical lines become more apparent and the canyon reveals its full character.

John Wesley Powell

The first recorded run of Cataract Canyon was made by John Wesley Powell and his crew in 1869 — one-armed Civil War veteran, geologist, and one of the great explorers in American history. Powell's party of ten men in four wooden dories had already survived weeks on the Green River before entering what Powell named "Cataract Canyon" in his journal on July 24, 1869.

Powell wrote of the canyon with a mix of awe and fear. The rapids were unmarked, the walls offered no escape, and the wooden boats were battered repeatedly in the drops. He returned in 1871 for a second expedition, this time with better boats and a photographer. His journals and the photographs from that trip gave the American public its first vivid picture of the canyon country of the Colorado Plateau.

Today the launch point at Moab and the takeout at Hite Marina (now largely submerged under Lake Powell) mark essentially the same corridor Powell descended. The walls have not changed. The river has.

Wildlife

The canyon corridor supports a remarkable variety of desert wildlife despite — or because of — its remoteness. Bighorn sheep are commonly seen on the canyon walls, picking routes along ledges that seem impossible from the water. Peregrine falcons nest in the cliffs above the river and can often be spotted riding thermals in the afternoon heat.

Along the banks and on sandy beaches, look for mule deer, coyotes, and the occasional river otter. Great blue herons fish the calmer eddies. The endangered Colorado pikeminnow and razorback sucker still inhabit the river, remnants of a native fish fauna that evolved over millions of years in the warm, turbid, pre-dam Colorado.

At night, away from any light pollution, the canyon offers some of the darkest skies in the lower 48 states. The Milky Way appears as a solid band overhead, and in late May, the Scorpius constellation rises in the southeast just after midnight.

Lake Powell & the Lower Canyon

Below the Big Drops, the gradient eases and the river slows as it approaches the backwater of Lake Powell — the reservoir behind Glen Canyon Dam, completed in 1966. Depending on reservoir levels, the flatwater section can begin as early as mile 20 below the confluence or as late as mile 40. In years of below-average snowpack, the lake has dropped low enough to expose canyon features that were drowned for decades.

The takeout at Hite Marina requires a motorized shuttle boat across the flat water of the lake. It is one of the more surreal transitions in American river running: from the roar of Class V rapids to the stillness of a desert reservoir, all within a few miles.
