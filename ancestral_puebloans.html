History & Culture of the Colorado Plateau

From Anasazi to Ancestral Puebloans

How a century-old term was reconsidered — and why it matters

The shift away from "Anasazi" is a story about who gets to name the past — and what happens when the people most directly connected to that past ask to be heard. It unfolded gradually over several decades and is still, as many visitors notice, incompletely implemented across the landscape.

Where "Anasazi" Came From

The word entered the archaeological literature largely through Richard Wetherill, the Colorado rancher who in 1888 stumbled into the cliff dwellings at Mesa Verde and became one of the first Anglo-Americans to systematically explore and excavate the Four Corners ruins. Wetherill borrowed the term from his Navajo guides, who used a version of the phrase Anaasází — a Navajo word whose translation has been debated but lands somewhere in the range of "ancient enemies" or "ancestors of our enemies."

Early 20th-century archaeologists adopted the term wholesale. It appeared in the foundational literature, was stamped onto park signs, and hardened into convention — for about a century.

The Problem With It

The issue is straightforward once stated: the people being labeled "Anasazi" have living descendants, and those descendants find the term somewhere between inaccurate and offensive. The Hopi, Zuni, Acoma, and the nineteen Rio Grande Pueblo nations are the direct cultural and biological heirs of the Four Corners cliff-dwellers. From their perspective, their ancestors had been given a name in the language of a different people — one that, in its most literal reading, casts them as enemies — by Anglo archaeologists who never thought to ask.

Pueblo elders and cultural leaders had been raising objections informally for years before the wider academic community began to seriously engage with those concerns.

The Turn: A Timeline

  • 1888

    Wetherill at Mesa Verde

    Richard Wetherill adopts the Navajo term Anaasází and uses it in correspondence and publications, seeding it into the archaeological vocabulary.

  • 1900s–
    1970s

    Term entrenched

    "Anasazi" appears in academic journals, NPS signage, textbooks, and popular media. The term becomes effectively synonymous with Four Corners prehistory.

  • 1980s

    Pueblo voices grow louder

    Tribal cultural leaders and the emerging field of Indigenous archaeology begin formally objecting to the term in professional settings. The American Indian Movement and tribal historic preservation programs give these objections institutional weight.

  • 1993

    Pecos Conference debate

    The long-running annual gathering of Southwestern archaeologists becomes a key venue for the transition. Pueblo representatives make a clear case; professional consensus begins shifting toward "Ancestral Puebloans."

  • Mid-
    1990s

    Academic adoption

    Major journals and university presses begin requiring or strongly preferring "Ancestral Puebloans." The shift in professional literature is largely complete within a decade.

  • 2000s

    NPS transitions — gradually

    Mesa Verde and Chaco Canyon update interpretive materials and major signage. Smaller, less-visited sites lag due to uneven funding and institutional inertia. Some original signs remain in place today.

Why the Park Service Lagged

Federal agencies move slowly, and the National Park Service manages dozens of sites with decades of signage, publications, brochures, and institutional momentum built around the old terminology. Physical signs at smaller or more remote sites often weren't replaced on any urgent timeline, and older publications remain in circulation. If you visited a site recently and saw "Anasazi" on a trailhead post, you were likely reading a sign that simply hadn't been updated yet — not a deliberate policy holdout.

A Note on Complexity

The Navajo Perspective

Some Navajo scholars and community members have argued that "Anasazi" — understood in Navajo usage as simply "ancient ones" — is not inherently derogatory, and that its erasure from the landscape is itself a form of cultural overreach. It is a minority position, but a genuine one that reflects the overlapping and sometimes competing Indigenous histories of the Southwest. The broader professional community has concluded that deference to the Pueblo descendants themselves should be the deciding factor — and their preference is unambiguous.

Where Things Stand

"Ancestral Puebloans" is now standard in academic archaeology, NPS interpretation, and most public-facing materials. The logic of the term is deliberate: it acknowledges living continuity rather than treating the culture as extinct and nameable by outsiders. It connects the archaeological past to present-day Pueblo peoples who maintain oral traditions, ceremonial practices, and direct kinship ties to those cliff dwellings.

The renaming isn't erasure of history — it's a correction to who gets to tell it. The descendants of the cliff-dwellers are still here, and they have a word for their own ancestors.
